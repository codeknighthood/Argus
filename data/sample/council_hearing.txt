CHAIR OKAFOR: Good evening, everyone. This public hearing of the Transport and Environment Committee is now in session. We are here to hear views on Ordinance 24-117, which would charge a daily fee for driving into the downtown zone. Each speaker has three minutes. Councillor Reyes, you proposed the ordinance, so I will start with you.
COUNCILLOR REYES: Thank you, Madam Chair. I support this charge, and I will tell you why. Downtown traffic has grown every year, and the average bus now crawls through the zone at nine miles an hour. A fee of eight dollars would cut the number of cars entering the zone, and every other city that has tried it has seen traffic fall. The revenue would pay for more frequent buses, which is the whole point.
COUNCILLOR BRANDT: With respect, Councillor, I oppose it. A flat fee hits ordinary families harder than it hits the wealthy. A nurse who drives in for a night shift pays the same eight dollars as a lawyer who drives in for lunch. That is not fair, and I do not think we should pass a policy that is not fair.
DR. LINDQVIST: May I add some data? The department modelled the charge over eighteen months. We expect traffic inside the zone to fall by roughly fifteen percent. I should say the model is less certain about what happens on the streets just outside the zone, because some drivers will simply park there and walk in.
CHAIR OKAFOR: Thank you, Doctor. Let us hear from the public. Ms. Adeyemi, please.
MS. ADEYEMI: I am a nurse at St. Mark's, and I want to answer Councillor Brandt, because I am exactly the person he mentioned. I support the charge. I take the number 14 bus to work when I can, but it only runs every thirty minutes after eight, so on late shifts I have no choice but to drive. If the fee paid for a bus every ten minutes, I would leave my car at home and I would save money overall.
MR. HALLORAN: I run a bakery on Ferry Street, and I am against this. My customers drive in from the suburbs, and many of them are older and cannot take two buses with a bag of bread. If they have to pay eight dollars to buy a six-dollar loaf, they will go to the supermarket on the ring road. I have been here twenty-two years, and I do not think my shop survives that.
MS. TANAKA: Mr. Halloran, I understand the worry, but the evidence from other cities points the other way. Shops in the zone did not lose customers after the charge, because people who arrive on foot or by bike come more often, even if they spend less each time. Our streets are also dangerous right now. Three cyclists were hurt on Ferry Street last year alone, and fewer cars would make it safer for everyone.
MR. PETROV: I drive from the east side because there is no train. I think the charge is a tax on people who have no alternative. Telling me to take the bus does not help when the bus takes ninety minutes. Raise the money some other way.
COUNCILLOR BRANDT: Mr. Petrov has made the point I was trying to make. The charge punishes people who have no alternative today. Improve the buses first, and then talk to us about a fee.
COUNCILLOR REYES: With respect, that gets the order backwards. The buses cannot be improved without the money, and the money comes from the charge. If we wait for better buses first, we will be waiting for years.
DR. LINDQVIST: One clarification, if I may. The ordinance as drafted includes a discount for low-income residents and an exemption for hospital staff on night shifts. That addresses part of what Councillor Brandt and Mr. Petrov raised, although it would not help Mr. Halloran's customers.
MS. ADEYEMI: I also want to say that the buses are the real problem. Every nurse I work with says the same thing: the late buses are too rare to rely on. Nobody wants to pay eight dollars, but nobody wants to wait forty minutes in the dark either.
MR. PETROV: Exactly. If I could rely on the bus, I would not be here.
CHAIR OKAFOR: Thank you. Mr. Halloran, you wanted to respond to Ms. Tanaka?
MR. HALLORAN: Yes. Other cities are not Riverton. We have long winters, and nobody cycles to a bakery in January. I would want to see numbers from a town like ours before I believe any of this.
DR. LINDQVIST: That is a fair request. The closest comparison we have is a northern city of similar size, where retail sales in the zone fell slightly in the first year and recovered in the second. I would not describe that as proof, only as a reasonable indication.
COUNCILLOR BRANDT: I would be more comfortable with a pilot. Run it for twelve months, publish the results, and let the council vote again with real numbers.
COUNCILLOR REYES: A pilot is a reasonable compromise, and I would support it, provided the revenue is ring-fenced for buses from the first day.
CHAIR OKAFOR: The clerk will note that a twelve-month pilot has been proposed. Is there anyone else who wishes to speak before we close?
MS. TANAKA: Only that a pilot is better than nothing. We have talked about this for six years.
CHAIR OKAFOR: Thank you all. The committee will vote on the pilot at its next meeting. This hearing is now closed.
